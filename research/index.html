RESEARCH REVIEW · 6 OCTOBER 2026

Chicago, October 1871

Recherche en français québécois ↗

A source-led foundation for an interactive map of the Great Fire. The Historical atlas has English and French Canadian editions, a clean vector map with historical geography, and source-checked incident cards inspired by the podcast.

Current status: The atlas has 31 researched landmarks, 204 additional named catalogue locations, and nine narrative checkpoints. Streets and waterways are approximately digitized from an 1868 map. There are also 613 traced building outlines/sections across 15 blocks from three reproduced 1869 insurance-map excerpts. Citywide building coverage is incomplete. Landmark symbols indicate locations rather than measured footprints. Ignition durations and a continuous fire perimeter remain unverified.

What the evidence changes

  • Separate ignitions matter. The Tribune’s October 11 report describes fires breaking out ahead of the main flames. Conzen’s reconstruction identifies a sequence of separate fires. A circular growth animation would erase this pattern.
  • Clocks disagree. The museum uses roughly 9 p.m. for the origin; Conzen uses 8:30 p.m.; the Tribune reports 9:30 p.m. Carter’s October 15 letter says about 11 p.m. These are conflicting reports, not four separate origins. Use a qualified time window and keep each attribution. Museum account · Carter letter.
  • Water Tower and pumping station need separate records. The tower survived. The pumping station was gutted but retained exterior walls. “Survived” must not imply its machinery still functioned. HABS record · University of Chicago Library.
  • A burned neighbourhood does not prove every building burned. The O’Leary cottage, Lind Block, and Ogden mansion require explicit exceptions.
  • The underlying map has a date of its own. Shober’s 1868 map is a pre-fire geographic source, not a complete inventory for October 1871. Later buildings and the LaSalle tunnel are added as separately sourced markers. We retain the old shoreline and period street names.

Chronology and place records

The complete, editable catalogue is stored in data.json. Each place has its source, period location, outcome, and a timing note. Each event records its evidence and uncertainty. The slider advances through nine milestones; its spacing is by chapter, not elapsed hours.

Milestones: evening context; the origin; the second ignition; the South Branch crossing; the Court House; the Main Branch crossing; the pumping works; northward expansion by noon; the aftermath on October 10. The initial preview is the Court House around 1:30 a.m.

Vector map and image provenance

Cartographic reference: Charles Shober & Co., Guide map of Chicago, 1868, University of Chicago Library, call number G4104.C6 1868 .C5. Scan obtained from the Wikimedia Commons image record, which links to the library catalogue and identifies the historical work as public domain. The local original is 4,792 × 7,792 pixels. This scan is retained for research and is not displayed as a map layer.

The vector streets, river banks, lakeshore and parks are an approximate manual digitization from the scan. They are stored as editable paths in vector-map.json. The local coordinate plane corresponds to the source crop at x=2,000, y=1,800, width=2,100, height=2,800, resized to 1,260 × 1,680. No modern OpenStreetMap geometry, tiles or historical-map image is loaded by the application.

Street centres, intersections, bridge alignments and water boundaries are simplified. This dataset is not georeferenced, and its accuracy has not been independently measured. Blocks are contextual land areas separated by streets; the tan building symbols are location symbols, not surveyed outlines. Street labels have fixed world-coordinate anchors; panning and zooming never recalculate their positions along a street. Collision handling may hide labels but does not relocate them. Nearby clickable place markers can be separated with leader lines.

Building photograph: Lovejoy & Foster, The Court House Shortly Before the Fire, stereograph, 1871, Chicago History Museum, ICHi-064281. Low-resolution reference from the museum’s Court House entry. Before public deployment, verify reproduction terms for the museum’s digital image or replace it with a confirmed reusable scan. We do not treat every photograph on a historical page as a pre-fire view.

Building outlines: coverage and source limits

The building layer contains 613 manually traced outlines or attached building sections in 15 blocks: four between Washington, Monroe, Dearborn and Wabash; eight between Huron, Ontario, Franklin and Dearborn; and three between Lake, Madison, Market and Franklin. Open the layer with “Explore building outlines” in the atlas. Every outline can be clicked or selected using the place menu. The buildings are vectors; the scanned source is not a map overlay.

The source is a reproduced insurance plan attributed to Sanborn, 1869, on Chicagology’s State and Madison page. The annotated scan is archived locally as assets/state-madison-1869.jpg. Its complete original sheet identity and institutional provenance have not yet been verified. Outlines, source coordinates and block transforms are available in buildings.json, generated by scripts/buildings.py.

Exterior outlines and visible party-wall boundaries were traced by hand. The additional excerpts use simplified rectangular extents for many small structures, so minor setbacks and wings are not reproduced exactly. Small roof features, internal divisions, very small outbuildings and ambiguous boundaries are omitted. The 613 features are not a count of independently verified buildings. Each block is fitted separately to our simplified vector street grid with a setback from road edges; shapes are approximate and not georeferenced or surveyed. Unlabelled structures retain generic names.

Additional plans: the Historical Society excerpt, covering eight North Division blocks, and the Central Block excerpt, covering three Market–Franklin blocks. Archived research scans: assets/north-division-1869.jpg and assets/market-franklin-1869.jpg. Trace coordinates refer to 2,048-pixel-wide renderings. New outlines and block transforms are in more-buildings.json. The Historical Society’s previous schematic rectangle is replaced by its linked plan outline; its 1871 landmark record remains available.

Named places: Seventeen museum-documented landmarks have been added, including the Nixon Block, Post Office and Custom House, Holy Name, St. James, Unity and Grace churches, the Arnold and Rumsey houses, and the McCagg complex. There are also 204 additional building or business-premises records located approximately from individual Chicagology pages. These use circular location markers, not fabricated footprints. A street address or intersection establishes a general location but may not establish a precise lot; block-middle positions and side offsets are schematic. The source’s reported lifespan appears in each catalogue card. We do not infer a fire outcome from an end date of 1871.

The source review inspected 426 catalogue entries (including some duplicate links and non-building subjects); 242 had map-image links, with 233 distinct image URLs. The source inventory records included entries and unresolved leads. Unclear dates, out-of-area locations, earlier demolitions and unresolved overlaps are retained as research leads rather than placed speculatively. Only three plan excerpts have been traced so far; finding an image link does not mean its buildings have been added or verified.

There are 848 selectable records: 613 plan features and 235 named places. This is not a census of 848 different buildings. Attached sections may be separate features; a named building may also occur in the 1869 layer; some records represent complexes or commercial premises. The 1871 catalogue and 1869 plan must be reconciled before unique-building counts can be reported.

A structure shown in 1869 may have changed by October 1871. The source page itself discusses intervening construction around State and Madison. This layer therefore states its date on the map and on each record, and never assigns fire outcomes or ignition times from the plan alone. Existing 1871 landmarks retain their independently sourced records. Further map sheets and cross-checks against 1871 directories are needed for citywide coverage.

Animated fire: illustration and evidence

The atlas now paints a translucent fire layer over its vector streets. The slider supports continuous scrubbing in both directions; playback takes five seconds per narrative interval. A bright edge indicates the illustration’s advancing front and a darker stain indicates its earlier extent. These intervals follow the nine chapters and are not equal spans of historical time.

The existing chronology supplies the broad sequence: origin near the O’Leary barn, separate South Division ignitions, the Court House, North Division spread, the pumping works, and northward movement. The animation uses separate plumes to represent fire appearing beyond the river. Its hand-authored boundaries, local arrival values, textured edges and cooling rate are visual choices, not digitized historical fire perimeters, a physical simulation, measured burning durations or building ignition records. The second-ignition chapter does not establish a plotted location for Bateham’s Mills. Fire beyond the northern edge of this reconstructed map is not fully represented.

The layer is labelled “Illustrative reconstruction” on the map and can be switched off. The lake and harbor are masked. River effects are restricted to a small South Branch area; the previously invented sequence of ignitions along the Main and North branches has been removed. Small clearings around the Water Tower, Lind Block, Ogden mansion and Nixon Block communicate documented survival, but are not surveyed surviving parcels. Other mixed outcomes, including the O’Leary cottage and McCagg conservatory, remain described in their place cards; tint on a district must not be read as an individual building’s fate. The paint never modifies the sourced status of any building.

River fire, corrected: PBS’s chronology links surface waste and oil burning to the late-evening South Branch crossing, which it places by about 11:30 p.m. The National Archives account establishes burning vessels and windborne material, but not a surface-ignition time. Water itself is not the fuel. We show small patches in the crossing area during the crossing/downtown chapters. Neither their extent nor their appearance/disappearance supplies a historical ignition or extinction time.

Whirls, corrected: There is no verified start time or track for “the tornado” in the sources consulted. Osier described tornado-like winds at the South Branch crossing. Frear described whirling fire looking along Washington toward the Opera House and State Street, without a precise clock time. These descriptions do not establish a meteorologically verified funnel at either pin. Two small, independent stationary symbols now indicate these reported scenes. The former travelling funnel and its arbitrary route have been removed. The South Branch pin marks a broad crossing area, not Osier’s established position; the State/Washington pin identifies the scene he described. Chapter assignments are editorial, not measured lifespans.

Explosions: An early newspaper transcription reports repeated gasworks explosions near Adams and Franklin after the Armory was reached about 2 a.m.; PBS puts the explosion by midnight. The map retains this disagreement. The transcription is headed Evening Post, October 10, but internally refers to the Ledger; the original issue identity needs checking. Goodspeed’s 1871 account, p. 147, reports O’Neil’s block demolished with powder at State and Harrison before the southward advance ended by noon. Its indexed scan text was accessible; direct full-PDF/OCR retrieval failed. This deliberate firebreak demolition is distinguished from the gasworks disaster. Burst sizes and animation speeds are symbolic. No exact blast radius, footprint, minute, or number of detonations is asserted.

Fire beyond the firefighters: The October 11 Tribune describes fire leaping beyond engines, surrounding equipment and forcing crews to retreat. A separate painted ignition and an ember arc now illustrate this mechanism in the West Division. They are not a surveyed engine line or a verified fragment trajectory.

Eleven clickable reports and stories expose their sources, time uncertainties and approximate locations. Their chapter windows mean “report grouped with this phase”, not “this event definitely started and stopped at these slider values”. The editable evidence catalogue is fire-incidents.json. The podcast inspiration is Charles Beauchesne’s Les pires moments de l’histoire: L’incendie de Chicago; the full third-party PodScripts transcript has now been read. It contains obvious recognition errors, and its approximate cue times have not been checked against audio.

Small details from the episode

The timestamped transcript was read in full on October 6, 2026. Story cards contain short original summaries and approximate listening cues, not reproduced passages. Five additions cover trees, heat, the cemetery/park, the lakeshore and the Court House bell.

Evidence checks: PBS’s narrative also reports exploding trees, but names no site and does not establish boiling sap as the mechanism (indexed text read; direct request failed). No measured location-specific temperature was established. Both topics appear as unlocated illustrated stories, not invented tree pins or a numeric heat map. Collected historical accounts describe refugees and survivors in the cemetery/park, with burned vegetation and possessions; Pamela Bannos questions the scale of open-grave stories. A blanket claim of deaths among everyone hiding in graves is not adopted. Lincoln Park is beyond this map crop, so its button opens a clearly labelled regional story without inventing northern geography.

The lakefront pin uses the approximate Ontario-area shoreline associated with Lambert Tree’s account in PBS’s chronology. The museum’s Court House record supplies the bell’s weight and collapse; no exact falling time is assigned. All drawings are symbolic, and episode timestamps are listening cues, not historical clock times.

The deterministic field is implemented in fire.js. Rewinding to a position reproduces the same paint. Labels remain above it, footprints remain clickable, and reduced-motion playback advances by chapter instead of continuously. A validated perimeter reconstruction and independently sourced building ignition intervals remain future research.

Decisions for the full prototype

  1. The Historical atlas direction has been selected. The other two layouts have been removed; the clean vector basemap replaces the scanned-image overlay.
  2. Georeference a suitable period map and reconcile 1868–1871 street and shoreline changes. Expand beyond the current 15 traced blocks using additional pre-fire insurance plans; verify the original sheet provenance and changes through October 1871.
  3. Validate landmark positions against a second spatial source. Keep the original Palmer House at State and Quincy separate from the State and Monroe hotel; resolve conflicting opening dates before giving its age.
  4. Recover and inspect the Conzen map; compare its broad phases with period burned-district maps and contemporary narratives. Record competing reconstructions where evidence differs.
  5. Represent time as intervals: earliest/latest reported ignition and independently supported damage/end intervals. Interpolated areas should be visibly labelled estimates. Never mark a building “burned out” simply because an arbitrary hour has passed.
  6. The continuous illustrative paint slider is implemented. Future building-level burning and burned states require independent ignition and duration evidence; preserve the current separation between animation and source-backed outcomes.
  7. Extend and validate the vector dataset north to Fullerton for the complete fire. Add further historical illustrations and human accounts after validating dates and reuse conditions.

The atlas supports keyboard-accessible place selection, timeline steps, play/pause, drag panning, wheel and pinch zoom, a fit control, and locating the current event. At small sizes the building panel stacks below the map. No modern comparison or podcast synchronisation is included.

Building colors and additional industrial scenes

Colors distinguish houses, barns and livestock premises, industry and storage, other researched uses, and unverified uses. Classification is explicit in the use register and relies on each record’s linked source and description. We do not infer an unknown building’s use from its neighbors, classify hotels as houses, or turn urban stables into farmland. Uses identified on an 1869 plan may have changed by 1871. These colors are not a combustibility score.

The October 8 Tribune account places Foster’s box factory behind Lull & Holmes among the losses of the previous night. Its marker appears only as aftermath at the start of the timeline. The account does not establish cardboard construction or a factory explosion.

The official investigation documents fire spreading to a match factory and Bateham’s mills after the Clinton/Mather church, then to Powell’s roofing establishment across the river. These are factory fires, shown with flame effects, not claimed explosions. Area pins are approximate; factory footprints and clock times are unresolved.

An October 9 dispatch reprinted in the Colonist reports demolitions around Wabash and Congress and places the stopping of the southward advance around 1 p.m. This is an additional demolition area, not a count of individual explosions; its broad chapter placement groups it with the State/Harrison firebreak.

Research is an evidence log, not a claim of complete historical certainty. Original summaries are intentionally short; follow each source for the underlying account.
